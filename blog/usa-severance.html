USA Severance Pay
The 2026 Authority Guide

Everything you need to know about US severance packages, from the industry-standard 1-week formula to complex federal WARN Act and state-level compliance.

In the United States, severance pay is a uniquely complex topic because it is governed by a combination of federal laws, state statutes, and private contract law. Unlike many European or Middle Eastern countries, there is no blanket federal requirement for severance pay in the US.

1. Federal Legal Framework: FLSA vs. WARN Act

The **Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA)** does not require severance pay. In most US states, employment is "at-will," meaning either the employer or the employee can terminate the relationship at any time for any legal reason without prior notice or payout.

The WARN Act (Federal)

The primary federal exception is the **Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) Act**. This applies to employers with 100 or more full-time employees. It requires a 60-day advance notice for:

  • Plant closings affecting 50+ employees.
  • Mass layoffs affecting 500+ employees, or 50+ employees if they represent at least 33% of the workforce.

If the 60-day notice is not provided, the employer is legally obligated to pay the employees back-pay for the duration of the violation (effectively a mandatory 60-day severance).

2. The Industry Standard: 1-Week-Per-Year

In the absence of a legal requirement, most American companies follow a "voluntary" severance standard to protect their brand and prevent litigation. The **"1-week-per-year"** formula is the baseline for most rank-and-file employees.

Standard US Formula Examples:

  • Entry-Level: 1 week of base pay per year of service (often with a 2-4 week minimum).
  • Middle Management: 2 weeks of base pay per year of service.
  • Executive Level: 1 month of base pay per year of service, often including bonus pro-rata.

3. State-Specific Nuances (Major Markets)

California: The High-Bar Standard

California has the most employee-friendly laws in the US. The Cal-WARN Act applies to smaller businesses (75+ employees) and smaller layoffs (50+ employees). Crucially, California courts have ruled that non-compete clauses in severance agreements are generally unenforceable, making CA packages more valuable to the employee.

New York: Extended Notice

New York’s mini-WARN Act requires 90 days of notice rather than the federal 60. It also covers businesses with as few as 50 employees. In NYC, specific industry-wide agreements (like those in the hotel industry) may mandate high severance payouts.

Texas and Florida: Strictly At-Will

Texas and Florida follow strict "at-will" doctrines. There are no state-level mandates for severance. In these states, your payout depends entirely on your original employment contract or the company’s internal handbook policies.

4. Taxation: The IRS Slice

Severance pay is considered Supplemental Wages by the IRS. It is subject to federal income tax, Social Security, and Medicare taxes (FICA). Most employers will withhold taxes at a flat rate (typically 22%) for supplemental payments, but your actual liability depends on your total annual income.

Pro-Tip: If you receive your severance in a lump sum at the end of the year, you may end up in a higher tax bracket temporarily. Some employees negotiate to have the payment "straddled" across two tax years to reduce the bite.

5. How to Negotiate Your US Severance Package

Many US employees don't realize that a severance offer is often a **negotiation**, not a fixed decree. Because the company wants you to sign a "Release of Claims" (promising not to sue them), you have leverage.

  • Health Insurance (COBRA): Ask the company to pay your COBRA premiums for 3-6 months.
  • Outplacement Services: Request the company pay for a career coach or resume service.
  • Reference Letter: Secure a "neutral" or "positive" reference letter as part of the agreement.
  • Unused PTO: Many states (like CA and NY) require payout of accrued vacation, but others do not. Ensure this is included.
